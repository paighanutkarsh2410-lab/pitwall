Engineering

How the pit wallis built.

A race goes in at the track and comes out on a screen, through two sites, six services and a link that can fail. Every number on this page was measured on this project.

One lap, end to end.

Verstappen completes lap 44 at Interlagos in 1:23.884, the lap he takes the lead. This is that event, carried the way every event is.

  1. Replayer sends it at the track, as it happened
  2. Uplink forwards it to the factory, through a link that can be cut
  3. Ingest checks it against the schema: 0.10 s after it was sent, p99
  4. Timing puts it on the board: 0.49 s, p99 (the board publishes once a second)
  5. The browser gets it in the next frame: one every 200 ms

Releases that roll themselves back.

Every service ships as a canary: one new pod beside the stable ones, judged on its own metrics before it is promoted. Three bad releases were shipped on purpose. Each was caught and removed with no one involved, and the stable pods served throughout.

A link you can cut.

The track and the factory are separate sites. Cut the line between them for a minute mid-race and the track keeps everything; when the line returns, the factory catches up.

Five promises, measured.

  • 99% of car telemetry reaches the pit wall within 500 ms
  • 99% of timing-board updates go out within 1 s
  • 99.9% of events pass the schema
  • 99.5% of pit-wall API requests succeed
  • 99% of link outages are caught up within 60 s

They are code, and the alerts are generated from them. In the first drill, an 800 ms delay injected into the API paged within a minute and cleared on its own once removed.

Run it yourself: clone the repository and type ./pitwall up. Or go back to the race, or open the pit wall.

Kubernetes (kind), Argo CD, Argo Rollouts, Redpanda, TimescaleDB, Prometheus, Grafana, Loki, Tempo, OpenTelemetry, KEDA, Chaos Mesh, k6, Toxiproxy. Services in Python; the pit wall in React.

Unofficial personal project. Not associated with Formula 1, the FIA, any team or broadcaster. Race data from OpenF1.
